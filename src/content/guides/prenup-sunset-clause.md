Short answer: **A sunset clause sets an automatic expiration date on your prenup, but using an all-or-nothing deadline introduces serious legal and emotional risks that many couples regret.**

For engaged couples who feel uncomfortable with the idea of a permanent prenuptial agreement, a sunset clause sounds like the ultimate compromise.

The logic seems romantic and fair: *"If we build a life together for ten years, we have proven our commitment, so the prenup should expire."*

While sunset clauses are legally valid across the United States, poorly drafted expiration terms create one of the most destructive scenarios in matrimonial law: **the cliff-edge divorce trap**.

## How a standard sunset clause works

In a traditional prenuptial contract, a sunset clause identifies a specific temporal milestone or life event:

- **Anniversary milestone:** The agreement dissolves on the couple's 10th, 15th, or 20th wedding anniversary.
- **Parental milestone:** The agreement terminates upon the birth or adoption of the couple's first child.

The moment that trigger occurs, the premarital agreement becomes legally null and void. 

From that day forward, the couple's finances fall under the default family law statutes of whichever state they reside in—either **community property rules** (where assets acquired during marriage are split 50/50) or **equitable distribution rules** (where courts divide assets based on judicial fairness).

## The dangerous "Cliff-Edge" divorce trap

Matrimonial attorneys routinely advise clients against hard, all-or-nothing sunset dates because of the perverse financial incentives they create.

Consider this classic family law scenario:

```
The 10-Year Cliff:
David and Sarah sign a prenup with an outright sunset clause at Year 10.
Under the prenup: Sarah receives a $50,000 lump sum and waives alimony.
Under state law (if prenup expires): Sarah receives 50% of marital business growth 
and long-term alimony, estimated at $1.8 million.

At Year 9 and 9 months, the marriage hits a rough patch.
David's financial advisors realize that staying married for 90 more days 
costs him over $1.7 million.
```

When an agreement contains an abrupt deadline, it introduces a ticking financial clock. An unhappy spouse with wealth to protect faces intense pressure to file for divorce months before the sunset date to lock in the prenup's terms. 

Conversely, an unhappy non-earning spouse might feel compelled to endure an unhealthy marriage just long enough to reach the financial finish line.

## A better alternative: the "Phased" sunset clause

Instead of creating a sudden legal cliff, sophisticated agreements use a **graduated or phased transition**. 

Under a phased schedule, the non-earning partner's rights expand gradually as the marriage endures:

| Years of marriage | Asset sharing schedule | Spousal support schedule |
|---|---|---|
| **Years 1 to 4** | All premarital assets remain 100% separate | Modest transitional support |
| **Years 5 to 9** | 20% equity in marital property appreciation | $3,000/month for 3 years |
| **Years 10 to 14** | 40% equity in marital property appreciation | $5,000/month for 5 years |
| **Years 15 to 19** | 60% equity in marital property appreciation | Formula-based spousal support |
| **Year 20+** | Standard state statutory equitable distribution | Full state statutory alimony guidelines |

A phased model eliminates the sudden incentive to file for divorce at Year 9, aligning legal incentives with genuine marital longevity.

## What happens to separate property when a prenup expires?

Couples often assume that when a prenup sunsets, everything immediately becomes 50/50. That is legally incorrect.

When a prenup expires, state statutory law takes over:
1. **Premarital assets generally remain separate:** Your pre-wedding investment balance or inheritance does not automatically transform into marital property simply because the prenup expired.
2. **Appreciation and marital earnings become divisible:** Any growth in retirement funds, home equity paid down with marital wages, and business expansion occurring during the marriage become subject to equitable distribution.
3. **Spousal support waivers vanish:** The court regains full statutory authority to award temporary or permanent alimony based on earning disparity and standard of living.
