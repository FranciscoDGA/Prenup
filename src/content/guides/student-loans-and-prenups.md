Short answer: **You do not inherit your partner's premarital student loans simply by signing a marriage license, but marital finances can easily expose your assets if you don't set clear legal boundaries.**

With millions of Americans carrying balances between $40,000 and $250,000 for undergraduate, graduate, medical, and law degrees, student debt is one of the primary reasons modern couples draft prenuptial agreements.

Without an agreement, everyday marital decisions—like refinancing, filing joint taxes, or using a joint checking account—can turn separate student debt into a shared financial burden.

## How the law treats student loans before marriage

Under standard American family law in equitable distribution states:
- Debts taken out **before the wedding date** are classified as separate property.
- Only the individual whose name is on the promissory note is legally obligated to repay the lender.
- In a divorce, courts rarely order a non-borrowing spouse to pay the other partner's premarital college debt.

So why do couples need a prenup for student loans? Because the real world is messy, and marital life quickly blurs the line between "yours" and "mine."

## 4 ways student debt becomes your problem

### 1. Refinancing or consolidating after the wedding
This is the most common financial trap. A couple decides to lower their interest rate on a private student loan. The lender offers a 2% rate reduction—on the condition that the higher-earning spouse signs as a **cosigner**.

The second you cosign or consolidate private loans into a joint facility, the premarital shield disappears. You are now personally and jointly responsible for 100% of that debt, regardless of what any divorce decree says.

### 2. The community property trap (CA, TX, WA, and others)
In the 9 community property states, all income earned by either spouse during the marriage is considered community property.

If your spouse uses their salary to pay $1,500 every month toward their undergraduate loans, they are using **community money** to pay off **separate debt**. In a divorce, family court judges can require the student borrower to reimburse the marital estate for those payments. A prenup allows you to waive these reimbursement claims explicitly.

### 3. Federal tax refund intercepts
If your spouse defaults on a federal student loan, the federal government does not need a court order to collect. Under the Treasury Offset Program, the Department of Education can seize federal tax refunds.

If you file a joint Form 1040, your portion of the refund can be seized to pay your partner's defaulted loans. While IRS Form 8379 (Injured Spouse Allocation) can help recover your share, it creates annual administrative headaches that a clear premarital plan avoids.

### 4. Graduate school debt acquired during the marriage
If your partner decides to attend medical school, dental school, or business school after marriage, loans taken out during the marriage are often presumed to be marital obligations unless a prenup states otherwise.

If the marriage dissolves shortly after graduation, you could find yourself arguing in court over whether you should contribute to loans that funded your partner's degree.

## How a prenup creates a student debt firewall

A properly drafted prenup establishes clear legal boundaries:

1. **Separates all past and future educational debt:** States that all student loans—past, present, and future—remain the sole personal liability of the borrower.
2. **Indemnification clauses:** Requires the borrowing spouse to legally indemnify (reimburse) the non-borrowing partner if a creditor ever attempts collection against joint assets.
3. **Waives reimbursement claims:** Confirms that regular monthly loan payments made during the marriage from marital funds are gifts, preventing ugly accounting audits in a divorce.
4. **Protects individual credit and assets:** Ensures that savings, individual investment accounts, and personal inheritances remain untouchable by student loan servicers.
