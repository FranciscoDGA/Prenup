Short answer: **Yes, a prenup can protect your 401(k), IRA, and pensions, but federal law requires a specific two-step process that many couples completely overlook.**

If you have built up a six-figure retirement fund, accumulated stock options, or have a lucrative employer 401(k) match, protecting those savings is often a top priority.

However, because 401(k)s are governed by federal law rather than state law, signing a standard prenup is only half the battle.

## The ERISA trap: why prenups alone fall short on 401(k)s

Most property division during a divorce is governed by state family courts. But employer-sponsored retirement plans—including 401(k)s and 403(b)s—are regulated by a federal statute called **ERISA** (the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974).

Under federal ERISA rules:
- Only a **current legal spouse** can waive rights to a participant's retirement plan benefits.
- A **fiancé is not a spouse**.

Federal courts have ruled that premarital waivers of 401(k) rights are ineffective under ERISA if signed before the wedding ceremony took place. If your prenup simply says *"Partner B waives all rights to Partner A's 401(k),"* the plan administrator may be required by federal law to ignore that clause.

## The required two-step solution

To properly protect a 401(k), family law attorneys use a proven two-step structure:

1. **In the prenuptial agreement (before marriage):**  
   The agreement explicitly defines the 401(k) as separate property. Crucially, it includes an enforceable contractual covenant stating that both partners agree to execute all necessary federal ERISA waivers **within 30 days following the wedding**.
2. **After the wedding ceremony (as legal spouses):**  
   You submit the official spousal waiver paperwork provided by your plan administrator (e.g., Fidelity, Vanguard, or Charles Schwab). Your new spouse signs the form before a notary public, fulfilling federal ERISA requirements.

If your spouse subsequently refuses to sign the post-marriage form, the prenup allows a court to compel signature or award financial offsetting damages.

## How IRAs, Roth IRAs, and Pensions differ

Unlike 401(k)s, Individual Retirement Accounts (**Traditional IRAs and Roth IRAs**) are not employer-sponsored and are **not governed by ERISA**.

Because IRAs fall under state family law, a standard prenuptial agreement waiver signed before marriage is fully effective. You do not need a post-marriage federal form to protect an IRA.

| Retirement asset | Governed by | Pre-wedding waiver valid? | Post-wedding action needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **401(k) / 403(b)** | Federal law (ERISA) | No (fiancé cannot waive) | **Yes** — official plan spousal waiver form required |
| **Traditional IRA / Roth IRA** | State family law | **Yes** | None required |
| **State/Municipal Pensions** | State statutory law | Usually yes (varies by state) | Confirm with state pension board |
| **Stock options / RSUs** | State contract law | **Yes** | Vesting schedule schedules attached to prenup |

## Premarital balance vs. active marital growth

Without a prenup, what happens to your 401(k) in a divorce?

Your pre-marriage balance is generally separate property. However, any contributions made after the wedding, your employer's matching funds, and the investment returns earned on those funds during the marriage are typically classified as **marital property**.

In a divorce, a judge divides the marital portion by issuing a **Qualified Domestic Relations Order (QDRO)**. A QDRO orders your plan administrator to carve out a portion of your retirement funds and transfer it penalty-free into an account in your ex-spouse's name.

A prenup allows you to avoid the QDRO process altogether by agreeing that:
- All active contributions and employer matches remain separate property.
- All future market appreciation and dividends remain separate property.
- Neither partner will seek a QDRO or division of the other's retirement accounts.
