Short answer: **Without a customized prenup, unvested stock grants (RSUs) and startup equity that vest during your marriage are legally divided as marital property under judicial time-rule formulas.**

For tech employees, software engineers, and founders in hubs like San Francisco, Seattle, Austin, and New York, equity compensation frequently makes up 40% to 70% of total compensation.

Yet most couples mistakenly believe that because a stock grant or startup equity occurred prior to the wedding date, it remains entirely separate. 

In American family courts, unvested equity is viewed as compensation for future labor performed *during* the marriage—making it prime territory for expensive divorce litigation.

## The RSU vesting trap: the judicial "Time Rule"

Consider this standard modern scenario:
- **Year 1:** You join a tech company and receive an initial grant of $400,000 in Restricted Stock Units (RSUs) with a standard four-year vesting schedule and a one-year cliff.
- **Year 2:** You get married.
- **Years 3 and 4:** The remaining 50% of your initial grant vests while you are married.

Under standard family law principles in community property states (such as California under the *Marriage of Hug* and *Marriage of Nelson* precedents) and equitable distribution jurisdictions:

A family court judge applies a mathematical **time rule formula**:
$$\text{Community Share} = \frac{\text{Months Married Prior to Vesting}}{\text{Total Months from Grant to Vesting}}$$

Because you performed work while married during years 3 and 4, the court presumes that the equity vesting in those years was earned by marital effort. 

Without an agreement stating otherwise, your spouse is entitled to an equitable portion of those vested shares—even though the grant was issued years before you walked down the aisle.

## Startup founders: the "Sweat Equity" problem

If you launched a company or hold early equity in a seed-stage venture, the legal exposure is even greater.

Under American business valuation jurisprudence (such as the *Pereira* and *Van Camp* accounting doctrines):
- If a premarital startup increases in valuation from $1 million on your wedding day to $30 million five years later, the court asks: *Did the valuation increase because of passive market forces or because of the founder's daily labor during the marriage?*
- Since a founder works 60-hour weeks during the marriage, judges regularly determine that this "sweat equity" belongs to the marital community.

A prenup provides a complete corporate shield by stating:
1. All stock, founders' shares, profits interests, and intellectual property remain 100% separate property.
2. The non-founding partner explicitly waives all claims to business appreciation resulting from active spousal labor.
3. The non-founding partner forfeits any right to inspect corporate books, sit on board meetings, or disrupt investor financing rounds.

## Handling Bitcoin, Ethereum, and cold storage

Cryptocurrency assets present three distinct problems that generic legal templates fail to manage:

### 1. The full financial disclosure rule
To make a prenup durable, the law requires **complete financial transparency**. If a partner conceals a Ledger hardware wallet holding $300,000 in Bitcoin or private key passphrases, an opposing divorce attorney can overturn the entire agreement on grounds of fraudulent concealment.

### 2. Forks, airdrops, and staking yields
If you hold 50 ETH before marriage and stake it on a validator node during the marriage, are the generated staking yields separate or marital income? A well-drafted clause confirms that all forks, staking rewards, yield-farming returns, and airdrops derived from separate crypto remain separate property.

### 3. Illiquid tokens and tax liabilities
Crypto capital gains incur substantial federal and state tax liabilities upon liquidation. A prenup must clarify who bears the tax burden if crypto must be sold or transferred during property division.

| Equity category | Default divorce outcome | Protected with a custom prenup |
|---|---|---|
