Short answer: **No law dictates who pays for a prenup, but etiquette and financial practicality usually put the cost on the partner requesting it or the higher earner.**

When one partner initiates the conversation or brings substantial assets to the table, expecting the other partner to spend thousands of dollars on attorney retainers often causes unnecessary friction.

Covering the other partner's legal costs is standard practice, but it must be structured properly so a family court does not view the payment as undue pressure.

## The 3 most common payment approaches

American couples approach prenup bills in three practical ways:

### 1. The higher earner pays the entire bill
This is the standard approach when there is a significant wealth disparity—for example, when one partner owns a business, has family trusts, or earns three times more than the other.

Because the agreement primarily protects premarital wealth or business equity, the wealthier partner covers the cost of drafting the document and provides funds for their partner's independent attorney.

### 2. A 50/50 equal split
When both partners have similar salaries, modest savings, or dual professional careers, splitting the cost evenly is common. Both individuals contribute equally to drafting and reviewing the agreement, reinforcing the idea of a shared financial roadmap.

### 3. Proportional split (income-weighted)
If one partner earns $150,000 and the other earns $50,000, they might agree to a 75/25 split. This mirrors how many modern American couples divide rent or mortgage payments during engagement.

## The legal risk: paying for your partner's lawyer

If you decide to pay for your partner's legal fees, you must navigate a critical legal boundary: **independent representation**.

In states like California (under California Family Code § 1615), spousal support waivers face strict standards of scrutiny if both sides do not have separate, uncompromised counsel. If you pay your partner's attorney directly, an opposing lawyer years later could claim that your partner's counsel was beholden to your wallet, compromising their independence.

To prevent this issue, attorneys use a **third-party payment agreement**:
- You provide the funds directly into your partner's retainer or bank account.
- The attorney signs a written disclosure stating: *"While Partner A is providing the funds, Partner B is my sole client. Partner A has zero authority over my legal advice, review, or negotiations."*
- Your partner retains full attorney-client privilege.

## How to bring up the payment question without tension

Money conversations during an engagement feel high-stakes. Use these practical scripts to keep the dialogue productive:

> **If you are the one asking for the prenup:**  
> *"I want us both to feel completely protected and clear on our financial future. Because I am initiating this, I want to take care of the cost—including paying for your own separate lawyer so you have uncompromised advice."*

> **If your partner asked for the prenup:**  
> *"I am open to having this conversation and going through the process together. Since my budget is focused on our wedding expenses, let's agree on how we will handle the legal fees so I can get proper independent review."*
