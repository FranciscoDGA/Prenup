You two found the house. The rate is good, the inspection came back clean, and your lender wants an answer by Friday.

Then someone says the word: *should we get a prenup before we buy this?*

Half of couples freeze at that question. One partner hears "I don't trust you." The other hears "we are about to tie our finances to each other for the first time in our lives, and nobody has written down what happens if it goes wrong." Both feelings are reasonable, and neither of them is the actual question.

The actual question is simpler and more boring: **which legal document does this house need, and when does it have to be signed?** Get those two answers right and the fight mostly disappears.

## The short answer

Yes, a prenup is one of the strongest tools for handling a house — but only if you are getting married, and only if you sign it **before** the wedding.

If you are buying the house **before** you are married, a prenup cannot help you yet, because a prenup by definition has to be executed before marriage. In that case you need a co-ownership or cohabitation agreement instead.

If you are buying the house **after** the wedding, a prenup is exactly the right instrument. It can decide who gets the down payment back, who keeps the house, and how mortgage payments and appreciation are divided.

So the order matters more than the document. Decide when you are buying relative to the wedding, and the rest follows.

## Why the timing decides everything

A prenuptial agreement is a contract between two people who are about to become spouses. It takes effect when you marry. That single fact creates three completely different situations:

**Buying before the wedding.** You are not spouses yet, so there is nothing for a prenup to attach to. You could sign the most detailed prenup in the world and it would govern a marriage you have not entered. What protects you here is a written co-ownership agreement — sometimes called the prenup of real estate — signed before or at closing.

**Buying before the wedding, then marrying.** This is the trap. You buy the house as unmarried partners, then marry, and the house can quietly change character. In community property states, property acquired during marriage is presumed marital, and funds you both put into it can blur the line between what was yours and what is now ours. A prenup signed before the wedding can prevent that by declaring how the existing house will be treated.

**Buying after the wedding.** Now a prenup works exactly as intended. You sign it before the ceremony, and it already tells both of you how a house purchased in year three of the marriage will be handled.

If you are reading this while already married, a prenup is off the table. You would need a postnuptial agreement, which most states scrutinize more closely because spouses already owe each other fiduciary duties.

## What a prenup can actually decide about a house

A well-drafted prenup does not have to be complicated. These five points cover most of what couples fight about later:

- **The down payment.** If one of you contributed $80,000 and the other contributed $20,000, does the larger contribution get repaid first before equity is split? If one side's parents gifted the money, is that a loan or a gift?
- **Mortgage payments.** Does the person whose paycheck covers the payment get extra credit toward ownership, or are payments treated as equal contributions regardless of income?
- **Title versus responsibility.** Being on the deed is not the same as being on the loan. Your agreement can state that one spouse holds title while both are liable on the mortgage, or the reverse.
- **Appreciation and improvements.** If the house rises from $400,000 to $650,000 over ten years, is that growth split equally, or does it follow the original contribution ratio?
- **What happens on sale or divorce.** Buyout price, who stays in the house during separation, and whether a sale is forced if you cannot agree.

The point is not to predict every scenario. It is to make sure that when you do disagree, you disagree with a document in front of you instead of in front of a judge.

## Title and mortgage are two different questions

This is where most couples get confused, and it is worth a paragraph of its own.

**Title** (the deed) decides who owns the house. **The mortgage** decides who owes the bank. They are recorded in separate documents and they do not have to match.

A common arrangement is one partner on the mortgage and both on the deed. That feels efficient at closing. It also means one person carries all the debt while both claim the asset — and if you split, the partner not on the loan has no ability to refinance and remove the other, while the partner on the loan is still personally liable.

Your agreement should answer both questions explicitly: who holds title, and who is responsible for the loan. Leaving either one to default rules is how people end up in partition lawsuits over a house they both thought they understood.

## What to put in the agreement

If you are drafting a prenup or a co-ownership agreement around a house, make sure these items are in writing and specific:

1. **The source of the down payment.** Dollar amounts and who provided them, including whether parental money is a gift or a loan.
2. **How ongoing costs are split.** Mortgage principal and interest, taxes, insurance, HOA fees, repairs, and planned renovations.
3. **Ownership percentages.** Not just "50/50" by default — actual percentages that reflect contributions if you want them to.
4. **The exit path.** Buyout terms, how the valuation is determined, right of first refusal, and what happens if one of you simply wants to sell and the other does not.
5. **Death or incapacity.** Who inherits the house, and whether a power of attorney is needed if something happens before closing.
6. **A review clause.** A date to revisit the numbers if your income or contributions change substantially.

Vague language is the enemy. "The house will be shared fairly" means nothing four years later. "Each party retains a right to reimbursement for down payment contributions made, in proportion to the amount contributed, before any remaining equity is divided" means something.

## What changes depending on your state

Prenups are governed by state law, and real estate is where the differences show up most.

Community property states — California, Texas, Arizona, Nevada, Washington, and others — presume that property acquired during marriage belongs to both spouses equally. California in particular has a reimbursement statute for separate property funds used toward a family home, and a prenup can modify how that applies.

Equitable distribution states — New York, Florida, Illinois, and most of the rest — start from a different place. The court divides marital property fairly, which does not always mean equally, and your agreement sets the rules before the court has to guess at your intentions.

Either way, the mechanism is the same: your contract overrides the default. The default is just different depending on which state you live in.

## When you do not need one

It is worth saying plainly, because most articles will not: sometimes the answer is no.

If you are buying a house together while unmarried and neither of you has meaningful assets, no children from prior relationships, and roughly equal incomes, a written co-ownership agreement is often enough, and even that is optional if you both intend an equal split.

If you are buying after marriage with matching incomes, no inheritance in sight, and a state where equitable distribution would land close to 50/50 anyway, the prenup may change very little.

What pushes it from optional to important: a large gap in down payments, one partner owning a business, money from family, property owned before the marriage, children from a previous relationship, or one spouse not working during the marriage. If two or more of those apply, the conversation is worth having before you sign a purchase contract.

## How to raise it without starting a fight

The objection you will most often hear is emotional, not financial: *if you loved me you would not need this.*

Reframe it. You are not planning a divorce. You are planning a purchase. Nobody calls an inspection pessimistic. Nobody calls title insurance a lack of faith in the seller.

The cleanest version sounds like this: **"I want us to agree on this now, while we're happy and nobody is upset, instead of during the worst moment of our lives."** That framing works because it is true, and because courts actually look for exactly that — voluntary agreement made without pressure, with time to review.

Give it time. A prenup presented three days before the wedding creates a presumption of pressure in several states. A prenup discussed six months out is just financial planning.

## The bottom line

Decide the timing first. Buying before the wedding needs a co-ownership agreement. Buying after the wedding lets a prenup do the work. Buying before and marrying after needs both a clear original agreement and a prenup that says how the existing house is treated.

Then put the specifics in writing: where the down payment came from, how payments count, who holds title versus the loan, and what happens if one of you wants out. Specificity is the entire value of the document.

Do it early, do it with separate lawyers, and do it while the conversation is calm. A house is usually the largest asset a couple will ever hold. Writing down the rules for it is not a lack of trust — it is the most ordinary kind of care there is.
