**Short answer: Yes, a prenup can protect you from alimony in most US states—but it is not an ironclad shield.**

Under the laws of roughly two-thirds of US states, a prenuptial agreement can legally waive, cap, or limit spousal support (alimony). If you and your partner both earn solid incomes, exchange complete financial disclosures, and sign well before the wedding with separate lawyers, a court will usually uphold a "no alimony" clause.

However, spousal support is the single most scrutinized part of any prenuptial agreement. Unlike property division—which divides assets you can already see—alimony governs whether a former spouse can cover basic living expenses years or decades in the future. Because of that, state legislatures and family court judges build strict guardrails around **prenup alimony waivers**.

## How alimony waivers work under US law (UPAA & UPMAA)

Most US states govern prenuptial agreements under either the **Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA)** (adopted in 28+ jurisdictions), the updated **Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act (UPMAA)**, or state-specific common law and statutes.

Under the UPAA framework, couples are generally free to contract regarding "the modification or elimination of spousal support." That means **can a prenup include no alimony?** In most states, yes—as long as the agreement meets two baseline legal standards:

1. **Procedural fairness at signing:** Both spouses signed voluntarily (no last-minute duress), received fair and reasonable disclosure of all assets, debts, and income, and had a meaningful opportunity to consult independent legal counsel.
2. **Substantive fairness at enforcement:** Enforcing the waiver at divorce does not violate state public policy—specifically, it does not leave a spouse unable to support themselves or reliant on government assistance.

For a deeper look at how different jurisdictions categorize waiver rules, see our companion guide on [whether a prenup can waive alimony](/guides/can-a-prenup-waive-alimony/).

## State-by-state nuances: Where protection is strong vs. restricted

Prenup alimony protection is not uniform across the United States. Where you divorce—not just where you marry—determines how much weight your waiver carries.

| State Category | Notable Examples | How Alimony Waivers Are Treated |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Broadly enforceable (UPAA / contract states)** | [Florida](/states/florida/), [Texas](/states/texas/), [Illinois](/states/illinois/), [Nevada](/states/nevada/), [Virginia](/states/virginia/) | Waivers and caps are generally enforced if voluntary and backed by full disclosure, subject to the public-assistance exception. |
| **Strict statutory guardrails** | [California](/states/california/), [New York](/states/new-york/) | Enforced only if strict procedural and fairness tests are met. California requires **independent counsel** at signing and a **7-day waiting period**; New York applies a two-stage "fair at signing, not unconscionable at divorce" test. |
| **Alimony waivers prohibited by statute** | [Iowa](/states/iowa/), [New Mexico](/states/new-mexico/), [South Dakota](/states/south-dakota/) | State law explicitly bars prenuptial agreements from adversely affecting spousal support. A "no alimony" clause is void even if both sides had lawyers. |

### The California rule: Independent counsel + 7-day waiting period

If you live in or move to **[California](/states/california/)**, two bright-line statutes govern spousal support waivers:

- **Mandatory independent counsel (Cal. Fam. Code § 1612(c)):** A prenup alimony waiver is *automatically unenforceable* unless the party against whom enforcement is sought was represented by independent legal counsel when signing. Signing a written waiver of counsel does **not** fix this for spousal support.
- **7-day waiting period (Cal. Fam. Code § 1615):** The signing party must have at least 7 full calendar days between receiving the final agreement and signing it.
- **Unconscionability at divorce:** Even with two lawyers and a 7-day wait, a California judge can still invalidate a support waiver if enforcing it at the time of divorce would be unconscionable.

## 5 reasons a court can invalidate or override an alimony waiver

Even in waiver-friendly states, a judge can set aside your alimony protection if any of the following occur:

1. **Public assistance override:** Under the UPAA and similar state laws, if enforcing a "no alimony" clause would make one spouse eligible for public assistance (such as Medicaid, SNAP, or state welfare), the court can order enough spousal support to keep them off government aid.
2. **Unconscionability after major life changes:** A waiver that looked reasonable for two 28-year-old professionals can be deemed unconscionable 18 years later if one spouse left the workforce to raise children, developed a disabling medical condition, or sacrificed their career to support the other's business. (If one partner plans to step back from work, see our guide on [stay-at-home parent prenup protections](/guides/stay-at-home-parent-prenup-protection/).)
3. **Hidden income or incomplete disclosure:** Failing to disclose a bonus structure, business distributions, trust income, or stock options is one of the fastest ways to have a spousal support waiver thrown out.
4. **Duress or wedding-week pressure:** Presenting a prenup days before the ceremony—when guests have booked flights and deposits are non-refundable—invites a duress challenge. Learn how to avoid this timeline trap in [signing a prenup 30 days before the wedding](/guides/prenup-30-days-before-wedding/).
5. **Lack of independent legal counsel:** Outside California, skipping a second lawyer isn't always an automatic disqualification, but when a higher-earning spouse drafts a blanket waiver and the lower-earning spouse signs unrepresented, courts view the clause with heavy skepticism. Read more in [do you need a lawyer for a prenup?](/guides/do-you-need-a-lawyer-for-a-prenup/).

> **Important boundary:** A prenup can **never** protect you from child support. Every US state treats child support as the child's legal right, and including a child-support waiver in your prenup can damage the credibility of the entire contract. See [what a prenup does not cover](/guides/what-does-a-prenup-not-cover/).

## Full waiver vs. capped/tiered alimony vs. duration limits

If your goal is financial certainty—not a courtroom fight 10 years from now—consider how different alimony structures hold up under judicial scrutiny:

| Alimony Clause Option | How It Works | Protection Level for Higher Earner | Risk of Being Overturned | Best Fit For |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Full Alimony Waiver (\$0)** | Both spouses permanently waive all post-divorce spousal support. | Maximum (if upheld) | **Moderate to High** (vulnerable to unconscionability & relocation) | Dual-income couples with similar earning power and no planned career breaks. |
| **Capped / Tiered Alimony** | \$0 for short marriages (e.g., under 5 years); modest monthly cap or lump-sum buyout that scales with marriage length. | High & predictable | **Low** (signals mutual fairness to a judge) | Couples with an income gap, business owners, or partners who may pause a career for family. |
| **Duration Limits** | Allows rehabilitative support (e.g., 1–3 years) to re-enter the workforce, but bars permanent or indefinite alimony. | High (eliminates open-ended liability) | **Very Low** (aligns with modern family court policy) | Marriages where one spouse may need transitional retraining or relocation funds. |

Family law attorneys frequently note that **a reasonable cap or duration limit is often stronger protection than a blanket \$0 waiver**. If a judge strikes down a \$0 waiver as unconscionable, you fall back to default state alimony guidelines—which could be far more expensive than a pre-agreed cap.

## Step-by-step checklist to make an alimony clause enforceable

If you want your prenup to reliably protect you from open-ended alimony, follow these six steps:

1. **Start 3 to 6 months before the wedding:** Give both sides plenty of time to negotiate without wedding-day pressure.
2. **Exchange complete, written financial schedules:** Attach signed schedules listing all income sources, bank accounts, retirement funds, real estate, business interests, and debts.
3. **Hire independent attorneys for both spouses:** Each partner should have their own family law attorney licensed in your state. This is mandatory in California for alimony waivers and strongly recommended everywhere else.
4. **Choose a realistic structure:** If there is a significant income gap or plans for one spouse to stay home with children, consider tiered caps or a lump-sum buyout instead of a harsh \$0 waiver.
5. **Include a severability clause:** Severability ensures that if a future court overrides your alimony waiver (for example, after moving to a state like Iowa or New Mexico), your property and business protections remain intact.
6. **Follow every execution formality:** Observe state waiting periods (like California's 7-day rule), sign before a notary, and keep signed originals with your attorneys.

## Bottom line

A prenup **can** protect you from alimony in most US states, giving you predictability over your future income and career. Just remember that courts enforce **fair, transparent agreements**—not one-sided traps. Full financial disclosure, separate lawyers, an early timeline, and a well-reasoned support clause (whether a clean waiver for dual earners or a capped/tiered formula for unequal incomes) are what turn a prenup from a piece of paper into real legal protection.
