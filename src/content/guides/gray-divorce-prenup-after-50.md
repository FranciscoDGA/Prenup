Short answer: **When marrying after 50, a prenup is an estate and healthcare shield rather than a divorce exit plan—it stops state probate laws from redirecting your lifetime assets away from your adult children.**

Younger couples draft prenuptial agreements to divide future earnings, manage student loans, and protect fledgling startups. When you marry in your fifties, sixties, or seventies, the reality is entirely different.

You already hold accumulated assets: paid-off real estate, pension benefits, 401(k) accounts, family heirlooms, and responsibilities to adult children or grandchildren. 

Without a carefully drafted agreement, standard state probate laws can completely dismantle your existing estate plan the day you say "I do."

## The biggest trap: state elective share laws

Most people assume their Last Will and Testament or living trust controls where their property goes upon death. In American probate law, this assumption is dangerous.

Almost every non-community-property state enforces a statute known as the **spousal elective share**. 

Under elective share statutes, a surviving spouse has a statutory right to claim between **30% and 50% of the deceased spouse's entire estate**, even if the will explicitly leaves everything to adult children from a prior marriage.

```
Example:
Robert (62) has $1.2 million in personal investments and two adult children.
He marries Linda (59). Robert's will states: "All my assets pass to my two children."
Three years later, Robert passes away in Florida.
Under Florida's 30% elective share rule, Linda can file a probate claim and 
legally take $360,000 off the top, directly reducing the children's inheritance.
```

To prevent this conflict, a late-in-life prenup includes an explicit **Waiver of Elective Share**. Both partners formally agree to forfeit statutory inheritance rights, allowing each partner's will, trust, and beneficiary designations to control 100% of their separate property.

## Healthcare, long-term care, and the Medicaid lookback

With healthcare costs in retirement easily exceeding hundreds of thousands of dollars, late-in-life marriages introduce immediate medical liability.

### 1. Spousal liability for healthcare debts
In many states, the "doctrine of necessaries" holds spouses mutually liable for essential living expenses, including emergency hospital care and medical bills incurred by the other partner. A prenup should include an indemnification clause clarifying that medical and assisted-living debts remain separate liabilities.

### 2. Medicaid eligibility and nursing homes
Here is an uncomfortable legal reality that most generic prenup sites fail to mention: **Medicaid does not honor prenuptial agreements.**

When a spouse applies for Medicaid long-term nursing home assistance, federal and state agencies evaluate the combined assets and income of both spouses, regardless of what your prenup says. If your new spouse requires memory care or skilled nursing costing $10,000 per month, state Medicaid rules may require your joint and individual non-exempt assets to be spent down before government assistance begins.

Couples marrying later in life must coordinate their prenup with long-term care insurance policies and standalone irrevocable asset-protection trusts.

## Social Security and pension survivor rules

Before setting a wedding date after 50, pay close attention to federal benefit milestones:

| Benefit | Rule if you remarry before 60 | Rule if you remarry at or after 60 |
|---|---|---|
| **Social Security Survivor Benefits** | Forfeited while married | Retained; you claim survivor benefits or your own, whichever is higher |
| **Federal/Military Pensions (SBP)** | Typically suspended or terminated | Frequently retained (check specific plan regulations) |
| **Employer Pension Joint & Survivor** | Cannot be altered post-retirement | Governed by original retirement election terms |

If one partner depends on a deceased spouse's Social Security survivor benefit of $2,800 per month, getting married at age 59 results in a permanent loss of that income stream until reaching the statutory age threshold.

## Protecting the family home without evicting the survivor

One of the most emotionally charged debates in remarriage after 50 is the primary residence:

- The owner wants the house to ultimately pass to their adult children.
- The non-owning partner worries about being evicted onto the street if the owner dies first.

A balanced prenup solves this through a **Life Estate Clause** or **Post-Death Occupancy Agreement**:
1. The agreement confirms the real estate remains separate property passing to the owner's children upon death.
2. The surviving partner receives a legal right to reside in the home for a defined period (e.g., 12 to 24 months, or for life), provided they cover basic utilities and insurance.
3. Trust funds or estate assets cover major property taxes or repairs during that occupancy window.

This protects the children's equity while treating the surviving partner with dignity.
