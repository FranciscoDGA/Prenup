Short answer: **Yes, a prenup can waive or limit alimony in most US states—but spousal support waivers are the single clause family court judges scrutinize and throw out most often.**

Even in states that explicitly allow alimony waivers, a total "zero alimony under any circumstances" clause can collapse years later if you skip mandatory legal formalities, hide assets, or leave your spouse financially destitute at divorce.

Understanding where state laws draw the line helps you draft terms that hold up rather than creating an expensive courtroom battle later.

## How state laws treat alimony waivers in a prenup

Property division clauses and spousal support clauses are treated differently under US family law. States generally fall into three categories when reviewing prenuptial alimony waivers:

| State Approach | Example States | What Happens in Court |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Waivers prohibited by statute** | Iowa, New Mexico, South Dakota | Prenup clauses eliminating spousal support are void or unenforceable regardless of disclosure or lawyers. |
| **Allowed with strict statutory guardrails** | California, New York | Enforced only if strict rules are met (such as mandatory independent legal counsel in CA or a strict "unconscionability" check at divorce). |
| **Allowed under UPAA / standard fairness** | Florida, Texas, Illinois, Arizona, Virginia | Generally enforceable if voluntary, backed by full financial disclosure, and not forcing a spouse onto public assistance. |

*Note: State statutes and appellate case law evolve over time. Always verify current rules in your [state prenup guide](/states/) before relying on a waiver.*

## The two biggest traps that void an alimony waiver

Even when both partners genuinely agree before the wedding that neither will ask for spousal support, two legal traps routinely invalidate blanket waivers years later:

### 1. The "public assistance" and extreme hardship override

Over half of US states have adopted a version of the **Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA)** or similar public-policy rules. Almost every version contains a built-in safety valve:

> If a prenuptial alimony waiver would leave one spouse eligible for **public assistance** (such as Medicaid, SNAP, or state welfare programs) at the time of divorce, the court has legal authority to require the wealthier spouse to pay enough support to avoid that outcome—no matter what the signed contract says.

Even outside public-assistance thresholds, states like **New York** review spousal support clauses twice: once when signed, and a second time at divorce to confirm the waiver is not "unconscionable" based on dramatic changes in health, disability, or earning capacity during the marriage.

### 2. Missing independent counsel (the California trap)

In **[California](/states/california/)**, Family Code § 1612(c) creates an absolute bright-line rule for spousal support waivers:

- **Independent lawyer required:** An alimony waiver in a California prenup is **automatically unenforceable** if the spouse waiving support did not have their own separate, independent attorney at the time of signing—even if they signed a written waiver saying they didn't want a lawyer.
- **Double review at enforcement:** Even with two separate lawyers, a California judge can still refuse to enforce a spousal support waiver if circumstances at divorce (such as a serious illness or 20 years out of the workforce) make enforcing zero support unconscionable.

Many other states stop short of requiring two attorneys by statute, yet judges still view a one-lawyer alimony waiver with intense skepticism. Read more in our guide on [whether you need a lawyer for a prenup](/guides/do-you-need-a-lawyer-for-a-prenup/).

## Three smarter alternatives to a blanket alimony waiver

If your goal is financial predictability rather than leaving a partner stranded after raising children or pausing a career, attorneys often recommend replacing a total waiver with one of three structured options:

1. **Marriage-duration tiers:** Set clear brackets instead of zero support—for instance, no spousal support for marriages under 5 years, temporary transitional support for marriages of 5–10 years, and defined caps for longer marriages.
2. **Income-gap or percentage caps:** Cap monthly support at a fixed percentage of the difference in gross incomes or a set inflation-adjusted dollar ceiling, eliminating open-ended litigation over lifestyle claims.
3. **Rehabilitative or lump-sum buyout:** Replace ongoing monthly alimony with a defined one-time transitional payment (or short-term retraining stipend) so both spouses achieve a clean financial break quickly.

Structured compromises look far fairer to a family court judge years down the road—which paradoxically makes your overall prenup **much harder to challenge**. ## Checklist before signing any alimony clause

- **Start early:** Never present a spousal support waiver days before the wedding; aim for at least 6–8 weeks beforehand so neither partner feels pressured.
- **Ensure full financial disclosure:** Attach complete schedules of assets, debts, income, and business interests so nobody can claim they waived support in the dark.
- **Fund independent attorneys for both sides:** Especially if one spouse earns significantly more or lives in a strict-scrutiny state like California or [New York](/states/new-york/).

## Bottom line

A prenup can waive alimony in most US states, **provided it follows your state's statutory rules, includes full financial disclosure, and avoids pushing a spouse into extreme hardship**. For most couples, setting clear, fair caps beats an aggressive zero-dollar waiver that invites a judge to tear up the clause later.
