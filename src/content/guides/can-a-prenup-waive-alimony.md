Short answer: **Yes, a prenup can waive or limit alimony in most US states—but spousal support waivers are the single clause family court judges scrutinize and throw out most often.**

Property division clauses mostly look backward at assets you already owned or acquired. Spousal support (often called alimony or spousal maintenance) looks forward at how two people will actually pay rent, buy groceries, and rebuild after a divorce that might happen 5, 15, or 25 years from now.

Because life changes dramatically over decades, a blanket "zero alimony under any circumstances" clause can collapse in court if you miss mandatory legal formalities, rush the timeline before the wedding, hide income, or leave your spouse financially destitute when the marriage ends.

Understanding where state laws draw the line helps you draft terms that protect both partners rather than creating an expensive courtroom battle later.

## How state laws treat alimony waivers in a prenup

US family law divides prenuptial alimony waivers into three broad legal buckets—from outright statutory bans to freedom-of-contract enforcement:

| State Approach | Example States | How Courts Treat Spousal Support Waivers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Waivers prohibited by statute** | [Iowa](/states/iowa/), [New Mexico](/states/new-mexico/), [South Dakota](/states/south-dakota/) | Prenuptial clauses eliminating or modifying spousal support are void or unenforceable regardless of disclosure, fairness, or how many lawyers reviewed the draft. |
| **Allowed with strict statutory guardrails** | [California](/states/california/), [New York](/states/new-york/) | Enforced only when strict rules are satisfied—such as mandatory independent counsel at signing in California or a "not unconscionable at divorce" review in New York. |
| **Allowed under UPAA or contract fairness** | [Florida](/states/florida/), [Texas](/states/texas/), [Illinois](/states/illinois/), [Nevada](/states/nevada/), [Virginia](/states/virginia/) | Generally enforceable if entered voluntarily with full financial disclosure, provided the waiver does not leave one spouse eligible for public assistance. |

*Notice how uneven that map is:* a clause that works cleanly in Florida or Nevada would be struck out on day one in Iowa or New Mexico. Check your [state prenup guide](/states/) before choosing how aggressive your support language should be.

## The three traps that void an alimony waiver

Even when both partners genuinely agree before the wedding that neither will seek spousal support, three legal traps routinely invalidate blanket waivers years later.

### 1. The "public assistance" and unconscionability override

More than half of US states have enacted a version of the **Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA)** or parallel public-policy doctrine. Almost every version contains the same built-in emergency brake:

> If a prenuptial alimony waiver would leave one spouse eligible for **public assistance** (such as Medicaid, SNAP, or state cash welfare) at the time of divorce, the court has legal authority to require the wealthier spouse to pay enough support to keep the other off government aid—no matter what the signed contract says.

Several states go even further than basic poverty protection. In **[New York](/states/new-york/)**, Domestic Relations Law § 236(B)(3) requires spousal support terms to pass a **two-stage fairness test**:

1. **Fair and reasonable at signing:** Neither spouse was coerced, misled, or kept in the dark about finances when signing the prenup.
2. **Not unconscionable at enforcement:** When the divorce is filed years later, enforcing zero support must not shock the conscience given health, disability, age, or years out of the workforce.

Think about two real-world couples with identical blanket waivers:

- **Scenario A (3-year dual-career marriage):** Two software engineers earning \$135,000 and \$150,000 divorce after three years with no children. Both kept full earning power. A court almost everywhere will uphold their alimony waiver.
- **Scenario B (12-year marriage with a career sacrifice):** One spouse leaves a \$90,000 nursing career for 12 years to raise three children while the other builds a \$400,000 medical practice, and later suffers a chronic health condition. Even under an otherwise valid prenup, judges in unconscionability-review states routinely set aside a zero-dollar waiver to award rehabilitative or transitional support.

### 2. Missing independent counsel (and the California rule)

In **[California](/states/california/)**, Family Code § 1612(c) lays down one of the strictest bright-line rules in the country for spousal support waivers:

- **Independent lawyer required without exception:** Any provision waiving or limiting spousal support in a California prenup is **automatically unenforceable** if the spouse against whom enforcement is sought was not represented by independent legal counsel at the time of signing—even if they signed a separate statement saying they waived the right to a lawyer.
- **7-day waiting period:** Under Family Code § 1615, the party signing must also have at least **7 full calendar days** between receiving the final agreement (or being advised to seek counsel, depending on representation) and signing it.
- **Second look at divorce:** Even with two separate lawyers and a proper waiting period, a California judge can still invalidate a support waiver if enforcing zero alimony would be unconscionable at divorce.

Even outside California, skipping separate lawyers is risky. When a wealthy partner hires an attorney and asks a fiancé to sign an alimony waiver unrepresented, judges see an immediate red flag of overreaching. ### 3. Moving to a different state after the wedding

Many couples draft their prenup in a waiver-friendly state like **[Texas](/states/texas/)** or **[Florida](/states/florida/)**, insert a "governing law" clause, and assume those terms follow them everywhere. Ten years later, they relocate for a job to Iowa, New Mexico, or California and file for divorce there.

Courts resolve relocation conflicts differently:

- Property rules often still follow the chosen state's law if there was a genuine connection at marriage.
- **Spousal support waivers**, however, tie closely to the new home state's public policy—because local taxpayers and courts absorb the fallout if a spouse is left destitute.

While a well-drafted choice-of-law clause improves predictability, relocation is one more reason **never to draft an overly one-sided support clause** assuming your wedding state's rules are permanent.

## Three enforceable alternatives to a blanket alimony waiver

If your goal is financial predictability rather than leaving a partner stranded after raising children or pausing a career, family law attorneys routinely recommend replacing an all-or-nothing waiver with structured terms:

| Clause Structure | How It Works in Practice | Judicial Durability | Drafting Complexity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Blanket \$0 Waiver** | Eliminates all temporary, rehabilitative, and permanent support forever. | Moderate (high overturn risk if careers/health diverge or no 2nd lawyer). | Simple, high-risk |
| **Marriage-Duration Tiers** | No alimony under 3–5 years; capped months/dollars for 5–15 years; fair ceiling above 15 years. | Very high (clearly reflects mutual fairness over time). | Moderate |
| **Lump-Sum Transitional Buyout** | Pre-agreed one-time payout (e.g., \$25k–\$75k scaled by years married) in place of monthly checks. | Very high (clean break, immediate predictability for both spouses). | Moderate |
| **Income-Gap Percentage Cap** | Caps monthly support at e.g. 20%–25% of net income gap for up to half the marriage length. | High (adapts automatically to future promotions or layoffs). | Higher (needs clear income definition) |

Why do structured compromises outperform total waivers in court? **Because judges look for evidence that both spouses thought about real-life fairness rather than trying to strip one partner of basic security.** Protecting core business equity or premarital savings while providing modest transitional support makes an entire prenup dramatically harder to challenge. ## What a prenup can never waive

When drafting support language, keep two bright legal boundaries strictly separate:

- **Child support:** No prenup anywhere in the US can waive, cap, or reduce future child support. Courts treat child support as a legal right belonging to the child, not something parents can trade away before marriage. Putting child-support waivers in a prenup doesn't just fail—it signals to a judge that the agreement was poorly drafted or coercive.
- **Temporary support during separation (in some states):** Even where permanent post-divorce alimony can be waived, several states still allow judges to award temporary *pendente lite* support and attorney's fees while the divorce itself is pending.

## Checklist before signing an alimony clause

1. **Start at least 8–12 weeks before the wedding:** Avoid the classic duress trap of handing over a support waiver the week invitations go out.
2. **Exchange complete financial schedules:** List every bank account, retirement plan, brokerage account, business valuation, debt, and annual salary—incomplete disclosure sinks waivers fast.
3. **Retain independent attorneys for both partners:** Mandatory in California for any alimony waiver, and strong courtroom insurance in all 50 states.
4. **Include a severability clause:** If a court strikes out an alimony waiver due to a state-law move or extreme hardship, severability preserves the rest of your asset protection.

## Bottom line

A prenup can waive alimony in most US states, **provided it complies with your state's statutory rules, pairs with independent legal advice, rests on full financial disclosure, and avoids pushing either spouse into severe hardship**. For most couples, setting predictable caps, duration tiers, or a transitional buyout delivers stronger real-world certainty than an aggressive zero-dollar waiver that a judge might tear up ten years later.
