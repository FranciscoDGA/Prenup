Short answer: **A prenup can divide property, protect debts, and set ground rules for spousal support, but it cannot touch children, personal behavior, or anything against public policy.**

If you include provisions that overstep statutory boundaries, a family court judge will simply strike them down. In severe cases, poorly constructed clauses can call the entire agreement into question.

Understanding what prenups cannot do keeps you from wasting legal fees and helps ensure your financial agreements remain durable under scrutiny.

## The 7 clauses family courts reject

American courts draw a hard boundary between financial agreements and personal or parental obligations. Here is what state laws across the US prohibit:

### 1. Child custody and parenting time
You cannot decide who gets the kids, establish a holiday visitation schedule, or decide where children will go to school in a prenuptial agreement.

Family courts retain exclusive authority under state law to determine custody based on the **best interests of the child** when a separation happens. A couple cannot predict their children's needs years in advance, so judges disregard premarital custody stipulations.

### 2. Child support waivers or caps
Child support belongs to the child, not the parents. State child support guidelines exist to ensure a child receives adequate shelter, food, healthcare, and education.

Any prenup clause attempting to waive child support, cap maximum monthly payments, or excuse a parent from college contributions will be struck down immediately.

### 3. Lifestyle and personal conduct clauses
Tabloids frequently report on celebrity prenups with clauses penalizing weight gain, mandating weekly date nights, or limiting visits from in-laws. In reality, American family courts refuse to referee marital behavior.

Courts consider these "non-justiciable personal promises." Judges will not hold evidentiary hearings on who failed to walk the dog or whether a spouse put on ten pounds.

### 4. Infidelity penalties in no-fault states
Clauses that award a financial bonus or impose a penalty if a spouse cheats are common requests, but their enforceability depends heavily on state law:

- In **no-fault divorce states** (such as California and Florida), courts routinely reject infidelity clauses. Under statutes like the California Family Code, the state explicitly prevents courts from judging fault or punishing emotional conduct during asset division.
- In a handful of fault-recognizing states, financial fault penalties face uphill scrutiny and must meet strict standards of reasonableness.

If your priority is financial security, relying on an infidelity clause often introduces unnecessary legal fragility.

### 5. Terms encouraging divorce
Under the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA) and general contract principles, an agreement cannot create financial incentives that actively encourage the dissolution of the marriage.

For example, a clause stating that a spouse receives an immediate lump-sum cash payout solely upon filing for divorce could be flagged by a judge as promoting marital breakdown against public policy.

### 6. Unconscionable terms leaving one spouse impoverished
While you can waive spousal support (alimony) in many states, courts will not enforce terms that leave one partner destitute or dependent on public assistance.

If one partner gives up their career to raise children and the prenup leaves them with zero assets and zero support after twenty years, judges in equitable distribution states can overturn the support waiver on grounds of unconscionability.

### 7. Provisions mandating illegal acts
Any contract clause that involves illegal conduct, fraudulent asset transfers, or tax evasion is void on its face. If a document attempts to shelter assets by falsifying tax documents or shielding proceeds from illegal activity, courts will refuse to enforce it.

## Summary: What belongs vs. what gets tossed

| Can be included | Will be rejected by courts |
|---|---|
| Separate vs. marital property definitions | Child custody and visitation schedules |
| Protection of pre-marriage businesses | Child support waivers or caps |
| Allocation of student loans and pre-existing debt | Infidelity penalties (in no-fault states) |
| Inheritance and gift protections | Weight, appearance, or lifestyle rules |
| Spousal support limitations (within reason) | Daily chore distributions |
| Retirement account and 401(k) division | Clauses encouraging divorce for cash |

## Why the "severability clause" matters

When lawyers or online drafting platforms assemble a prenup, they include standard contractual safeguards. The most critical is the **severability clause**.

A severability clause explicitly tells the judge: *"If any single paragraph in this agreement is found unlawful or unenforceable, remove that paragraph and enforce everything else."*

Without a severability provision, an overreaching or invalid clause could lead an aggressive divorce attorney to argue that the entire document should be thrown out.
