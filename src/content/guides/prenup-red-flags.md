Short answer: **If a prenup feels like an ultimatum, hides assets, rushes you before the wedding, or demands you share a lawyer, it is carrying major red flags that courts may reject.**

A proper premarital contract is designed to protect both partners and establish financial clarity. When an agreement is aggressively one-sided or negotiated under duress, it creates deep emotional strain and often fails under courtroom scrutiny.

Knowing what red flags look like helps you spot bad agreements early and take the necessary steps to safeguard your interests.

## 1. Rushed timing right before the ceremony

Handing your partner an agreement two weeks—or two days—before guests arrive for the wedding is a classic recipe for legal trouble.

When one partner faces non-refundable venue deposits, family travel arrangements, and social embarrassment, signing a legal contract under that pressure can be interpreted as **coercion and duress**.

- In California, the state legislature enacted the **7-day rule** (California Family Code § 1615). An unrepresented partner must have at least 7 complete calendar days between receiving the final draft and signing it. If this rule is broken, the agreement faces immediate legal jeopardy.
- In other states, judges look closely at the calendar. A document signed the morning of the wedding will be viewed with extreme skepticism.

If you are dealing with a compressed calendar, read our breakdown of [signing a prenup 30 days before the wedding](/guides/prenup-30-days-before-wedding/).

## 2. Using the same attorney (dual representation)

Trying to save money by having one lawyer represent both of you is a trap.

In the United States, professional ethics rules prohibit an attorney from representing both sides of an adverse negotiation. A single lawyer cannot simultaneously advocate for the asset protection of one client while fighting for the support rights of the other.

If an attorney claims they can "represent both of you neutrally," walk away. While platforms like <a href="https://helloprenup.com/?via=francisco" rel="sponsored noopener">HelloPrenup</a> allow couples to draft terms collaboratively online, any formal legal review requires **separate, independent attorneys** for each person.

## 3. Incomplete financial disclosures

Under the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA), every valid prenup requires a full and fair disclosure of:
- All bank, brokerage, and retirement accounts
- Real estate holdings and mortgages
- Business ownership, partnerships, and equity options
- Debts, tax liabilities, and outstanding personal loans

If your fiancé glosses over their true net worth, refuses to attach tax returns, or writes "value unknown" next to major assets, that is a severe red flag. Hidden assets eliminate the "informed" aspect of legal consent, giving judges solid grounds to strike the agreement down during a divorce.

## 4. Total waiver of support with zero safety net

It is common for higher earners to seek limits on spousal support (alimony). However, demanding a total and permanent waiver regardless of marital duration or health circumstances is a warning sign.

Imagine one spouse leaves the workforce for fifteen years to raise three children while the other spouse's earnings climb into seven figures. A clause leaving the stay-at-home parent with zero alimony and zero marital property is considered **unconscionable** by many family courts.

Balanced agreements often include sunset clauses, tiered support structures based on years of marriage, or lump-sum transitional payments.

## 5. Bizarre lifestyle demands or penalties

Be wary of agreements that attempt to control personal or domestic habits. Clauses that penalize weight gain, mandate sexual frequency, restrict visiting relatives, or assign kitchen duties do not belong in legal contracts.

As explained in our guide on [what a prenup cannot cover](/guides/what-does-a-prenup-not-cover/), family courts refuse to enforce lifestyle penalties. Including them clutters the document and signals to a judge that the agreement was negotiated with unreasonable control.

## 6. "Take it or leave it" ultimatums

Negotiation is the essence of a durable contract. If your partner refuses to discuss individual provisions, dismisses your concerns, or threatens to cancel the wedding if you ask for adjustments, you are not engaging in a mutual agreement.

A healthy prenup process involves trade-offs. If one partner protects a premarital business, the other partner might receive a defined housing allowance or a share of joint retirement growth.

## Red flags vs. healthy terms

| Red flag warning | Healthy agreement standard |
|---|---|
| Delivered 48 hours before the wedding | Negotiated 3 to 6 months in advance |
| One attorney representing both partners | Separate, independent counsel for each partner |
| Estimated or hidden financial numbers | Complete tax returns and verified account balances |
| Total waiver leaving one partner with nothing | Proportional protections with transitional support |
| Lifestyle rules and personal conduct penalties | Strict focus on financial assets, debts, and property |
| Non-negotiable ultimatum | Open dialogue with reasonable compromise |

## What to do if you spot these warning signs

If your partner presents an agreement that sets off alarm bells:

1. **Pause the signing:** Do not let wedding deadlines force you into signing a document you do not understand or agree with.
2. **Hire your own counsel:** Never sign an agreement without your own lawyer reviewing every clause. If cost is a barrier, discuss flat-fee alternatives.
