Let's name the fear precisely: you're not afraid of the *document*. You're afraid of spending $700, planning around it for two years, and then having a judge in a courtroom wave it off with *"this isn't worth the paper."*

That fear is the last real objection to doing this — so let's kill it with the actual rules, not marketing reassurance.

## The good news the industry undersells

**No court in America invalidates a prenup because it was made online.**

Not one. Electronic signatures have been federally recognized since the ESIGN Act of 2000 (and state UETA laws before that). Courts don't ask where your document was typeset — they ask how it was signed. "The software did it" has never appeared on a list of reasons a judge set aside an agreement.

Meanwhile, lawyers' $5,000 prenups get tossed every week — for process failures that had nothing to do with the price tag.

Where you got it doesn't decide your fate. **How you execute it does.**

## The 5 things courts actually check

### 1. Full financial disclosure — the #1 killer

Both of you listed assets, debts, and income **in writing, attached to the agreement, signed.** Hidden a $400,000 account? That alone can void the entire thing — no matter who drafted it.

Good platforms *force* this: you can't proceed without completing both schedules. Try that with an unsigned pile of PDFs.

### 2. Genuine voluntariness — no duress

Signed freely, with time to think, no threats to call off the wedding, no last-minute ambush. Courts look hardest at **timing**: documents presented 72 hours before the wedding smell like coercion even when they aren't.

Start early (3–6 months) and the duress argument never gets a foothold.

### 3. Timing before the wedding

Both parties must sign **before** the ceremony. Post-wedding changes become a different, harder instrument (a postnup) that gets scrutinized more closely. This one is binary: before the aisle, or you're in a different legal universe.

### 4. Your state's formalities

Written (always), signed by both (always), plus whatever your state adds: **notarization, witnesses, specific statutory language.** Reputable platforms build your state's requirements into the signing ceremony — but only if you follow it exactly. Skipping the notary your state requires is how cheap documents die.

### 5. Terms that aren't unconscionable

Courts rarely second-guess substantive fairness — prenups don't have to be "equal." But terms that are shocking **at signing** (one spouse left with nothing while the other keeps everything, stacked with hidden disclosure) give judges a reason to intervene. Fair process protects unfair-ish terms; unfair process protects nothing.

## Where DIY genuinely fails (and where it wins)

**Online platforms win when:** finances are standard — a house, accounts, retirement, debt, maybe kids from before. Both of you answer the same guided questions, state clauses load automatically, disclosures are mandatory, and the signing ceremony includes your state's notary/witness steps. That's a better *process* than most lawyers provide for the money.

**You need a lawyer when:**
- A business, partnership interest, or intellectual property needs valuation clauses
- One side has **far** more assets or income (fairness drafting gets subtle)
- Trusts, inheritance structures, or real estate in multiple states are involved
- One spouse isn't fluent in the language being signed
- You want aggressive support waivers or unusual estate terms

The realistic hybrid — and what most careful couples do — is **platform for structure, lawyer for the two or three hard clauses.** You pay an attorney for two hours instead of thirty.

## The 30-second validity check before you sign

Before the wedding day, confirm all five:

1. ☐ Both disclosure schedules complete and attached?
2. ☐ Signed willingly, weeks ahead — not under deadline pressure?
3. ☐ Signed before the ceremony?
4. ☐ Notary/witness steps for **your state** completed on camera/in person as required?
5. ☐ Neither of you reading it for the first time at signing?

Five checkmarks. That's the whole ballgame — no courthouse cares about anything else.

## The honest bottom line

Online prenups are valid because *process* is valid — and process is something you control completely. The platforms in this space built their businesses on enforceability, because one tossed agreement destroys their reviews.
