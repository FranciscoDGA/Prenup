## Why you're dreading it (name the fear first)

You're not afraid of paperwork. You're afraid of the look.

Specifically: the flicker that says *so you've been thinking about us failing.* The pause where they do math about what you're really saying. The conversation you'll never get back to normal after.

That fear is why most couples skip it — and why they arrive at a courthouse ten years later with nothing agreed, dividing a house they both bled for.

The dread is lying to you about what this conversation means. Your job is to walk in knowing what it means, so they can't misread it.

## The frame that changes everything

You are not saying: *"In case we divorce…"*
You are saying: *"I want to do this right."*

Two people who have already survived weddings, leases, and family group chats decide to start married life with **zero ambiguity about money** — what's ours, what's yours, and how any future twist (a business, a baby, a parent moving in) gets handled.

That's it. That's the whole pitch. You're not preparing to leave; you're removing the only subject couples lie to each other about.

> **Trust your partner out loud.** The couples who say the hard sentence in year zero don't have more marriages — they have fewer resentments.

## The timing rules (non-negotiable)

- **3–6 months before the wedding.** Early enough to be calm, early enough that both sides have counsel if needed, early enough it never smells like an ultimatum.
- **Never during a fight.** "We should get a prenup" after a money argument is a grenade. Never, ever.
- **Never the final week.** Last-minute signing is the second-leading reason prenups get tossed — courts call it duress. Kindness and enforceability want the same thing here.
- **Pick a boring Sunday morning,** not dinner with witnesses. No audience, no clock.

## The four scripts (copy them)

### Script 1 — the universal opener

> *"I've been thinking about how to do this marriage right, and I read that couples who agree on money before the wedding fight about it less after. Can we look at something together this week? Not a contract — a tool. If we don't like it, we don't use it."*

Note what's missing: the word *divorce*, the word *protect myself*, and any document you already prepared.

### Script 2 — if you earn more (or have more saved)

> *"I want to build this life with you, and I also don't want a shadow over anything. It wouldn't feel right to have assets you didn't see or terms you didn't shape. So let's do this together — we both answer the same questions, and nothing goes in that we didn't both pick."*

This script kills the one-sided feeling before it starts. *We both answer the same questions* is doing the heavy lifting.

### Script 3 — if they have debt, or a business, or kids from before

> *"This isn't about doubting you — it's about making sure nothing from before us follows either of us by accident. Your student loans stay yours. Your business stays your business. My kids' situation stays clear. I'd rather us decide that now, calmly, than have a judge guess someday."*

Notice: you're protecting **them** first. Debt and pre-marital property are where silent unfairness lives — and pointing at the silent unfairness, not at them, is the whole trick.

### Script 4 — when they ask the direct question: *"Do you think we'll fail?"*

> *"No. I think we'll succeed, and I want us to succeed with nothing hanging over us. Every couple I know who went through a bad divorce said the same thing: 'We never talked about it when we still liked each other.' I want to talk about it now — while we still like each other."*

That last line lands. It's warm, it's honest, and it's impossible to hear as an insult.

## If it still goes badly

**Reaction 1: Hurt.** Don't spiral. *"I get why that landed badly. Can I show you what I actually mean tomorrow?"* — then let a neutral tool explain it. Sometimes the messenger is the problem; a guided platform becomes the messenger.

**Reaction 2: Silence.** People stall when they feel ambushed. Give it two days, then try the shared-tool frame: *"Can we just answer a few questions together and see?"* Curiosity survives where pressure doesn't.

**Reaction 3: Flat refusal to ever discuss it.** This is the only genuinely alarming response — not because a prenup is mandatory, but because a marriage is forty years of money conversations, and this is the easiest one you'll ever have. If the easiest one is forbidden, get a neutral third party (a counselor, a mediator) involved before the wedding, not after.

## The cheat code nobody uses

You don't have to be the person holding the contract.

Online prenup platforms exist precisely so the awkward questions come from **a screen instead of you**: *"What should happen to the house if you separate?"* Both of you answer. Both of you see the other's answer. The document assembles itself from your shared choices.

You stop being the partner who "wanted a prenup" and become the partner who *figured out an easier way.* That's the difference between a fight and a form.

## The four sentences, one more time

1. "I want to do this marriage right — can we look at something together?"
2. "We both answer the same questions. Nothing goes in that we didn't both pick."
3. "Nothing from before us should follow us by accident."
4. "I'd rather talk about it now, while we still like each other."

Say one of them this week. The twelve seconds are the whole price — and everything after them gets easier.
