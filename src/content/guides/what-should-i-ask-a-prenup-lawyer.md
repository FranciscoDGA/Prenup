Short answer: **ask about their experience with prenups specifically, how they charge, the timeline, what disclosure they'll require, and how they handle disagreements between partners.** A first consultation is also where you find out whether the lawyer is a fit — and whether their answers make you more confident or less.

Here's a working list, grouped the way a first meeting usually flows.

## Before you book

Look for family-law or matrimonial experience specifically — prenups sit inside divorce and property law, not general practice. Ask upfront:

1. **Do you draft and review prenups regularly?** How many in the past year?
2. **Are you licensed in the state where we'll marry** (or where we live)?
3. **Do both partners need separate lawyers, and how do you coordinate** with the other side's counsel?

Independent counsel for each partner is what makes an agreement durable. If a lawyer discourages you from having your own representation, treat that as a red flag — not a money-saving tip.

## Questions about fees

4. **Hourly or flat fee?** Which one applies to a standard prenup?
5. **What's included in the fee** — drafting, revisions, negotiation rounds, signing?
6. **What isn't included** — filing, notarization, the other side's edits?
7. **Who pays, and does it matter** if our asset situations are very different?

One national survey pegged average lawyer hourly rates around $314, and reported prenup totals from roughly $1,000 to $10,000+ per partner. Flat fees are increasingly common for straightforward agreements. Never accept a vague estimate — ask what triggers additional charges.

## Questions about the process

8. **What's the realistic timeline from first draft to signing?**
9. **Who drafts first** — and does that side's lawyer set the tone?
10. **What financial disclosure will you require from each of us?**
11. **How do you handle disagreements** between partners about terms?
12. **Does our state have any waiting period or formalities** (witnesses, notarization) we need to plan for?

Disclosure is the part couples try to shortcut — and it's the part courts inspect. Ask exactly what counts as sufficient: account statements? business valuations? a sworn list? A lawyer who waves disclosure aside is building something fragile.

## Questions about enforceability and the future

13. **What typically makes prenups fail** in this state's courts?
14. **Which terms are risky or unenforceable here?** (Support waivers, child-related terms, and clauses about the marriage itself are common trouble spots.)
15. **How would we change the agreement later** — amendments, reviews, what does that cost?
16. **What happens if we move to another state** — does the prenup travel with us?
17. **Is there anything in our situation** — a business, a pension, an inheritance, children from a prior marriage — that needs special language?

These answers reveal whether the lawyer is actually practicing in your state or just reciting generic advice.

## Bring this to the meeting

- A list of assets: bank and investment accounts, real estate, business interests, retirement, vehicles
- A list of debts: student loans, credit cards, mortgages, anything co-signed
- Income details for both partners
- Your wedding date and a target signing date
- Anything you already know your partner cares about protecting

Arriving with this turns a vague consultation into a concrete one — and often a cheaper one.

## Red flags, in one place

- Guarantees: "this will never be challenged" — no lawyer can promise that
- Pressure to sign before you've had time to read and ask questions
- Discouragement of independent counsel for your partner
- Fees that stay verbal
- No family-law background
