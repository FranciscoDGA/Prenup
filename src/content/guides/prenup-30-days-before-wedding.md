You looked at the calendar just now.

The invitations went out weeks ago, the venue is paid, and somewhere between venue tours and seating charts, the thought finally surfaced: *we don't have a prenup.* Maybe it's the house you're bringing in. Maybe the business. Maybe the quiet math about your student loans or their savings.

Thirty days. Here's the truth you need, delivered calmly: **this is tight, but it is not impossible — and panicking is the one thing that would actually sink it.**

## Why speed is dangerous (and how to be fast without being reckless)

Family courts examine one thing above all when agreements are challenged: **was it voluntary?** And nothing smells like coercion like a prenup presented in the final days before the wedding. Last-minute signing is the **second-leading reason** prenups get set aside nationwide — not because the content was wrong, but because the *clock* did the talking.

So the entire strategy for a 30-day prenup is: **move fast through process, never through pressure.** Your fiancé must feel they had time, not that time was used against them. Same speed on your side; completely different experience on theirs.

## The 30-day plan (start today, not tomorrow)

**Days 1–3 — Disclosure, both of you, simultaneously.**
Bank accounts, retirement, debts, income, inheritances. Lists, not novels. Doing this on day one does two things: it's legally the foundation of the whole agreement, and it signals *"we're moving because the wedding is close, not because I'm hiding anything."*

**Days 4–10 — Draft the simple version on a platform.**
Guided tools (state-specific, disclosure-forced) compress what took lawyers two weeks into evenings. Keep the scope lean — see the cut list below. You're aiming for 6–10 pages, not 30.

**Days 11–20 — Real review.**
Your partner reads every line. If they want a lawyer's glance at their own questions, this is the window — even a two-hour consult satisfies the "independent counsel" gold standard for most simple cases. **Do not shorten this phase.** This is the part courts will examine later; everything else can be fast.

**Days 21–25 — Signing ceremony.**
Notary (and witnesses if your state requires them) scheduled and completed. Online notarization is legal in a growing list of states — confirm yours. Sign **at least a week before the wedding**, not the day before.

**Days 26–30 — Buffer.**
Documents stored, copies exchanged, beneficiaries and account titles checked. Then you're done, and the remaining weeks belong to the wedding.

## The cut list: what stays when time is short

Keep only what prevents future fights:

✅ **Separate property** — what each of you brought in stays yours
✅ **The house** — ownership, payments, what happens if you sell
✅ **Premarital debts** — assigned, each to their owner
✅ **Basic support terms** — cap or structure, within your state's limits

Defer (they belong in a postnup, calmly, after the honeymoon):

⏸ Business valuation clauses
⏸ Life-insurance schedules
⏸ Retirement division details
⏸ Sunset clauses, morality clauses, pet clauses

A short agreement **both of you understood completely** beats a comprehensive one signed in a blur. Courts enforce clarity, not volume.

## The conversation you have to get right

Your partner deserves a heads-up that respects the awkwardness of the timing:

> *"I should have raised this months ago — that's on me. The wedding is close, and I don't want to start our marriage with this hanging over us. Here's what I'm proposing: we lock down just the basics — what we each brought in, the house, the debts — fast and fairly. Anything complex, we do together after we're married. Can we start tonight?"*

Notice what changed: **you own the delay**, you're asking for the *small* version, and you've already scheduled the rest for later. That's not an ambush; that's a cleanup.

## If the answer is "there isn't time"

Then **do not force it.** A signature extracted in the final week is the signature most likely to be thrown out — you'd have spent the month building paper that fails exactly when tested.

Instead:

1. **Keep finances separate until after the wedding.** Don't merge accounts or title changes in the interim.
2. **Agree on a postnup date in writing** — "we'll complete this within 60 days of the wedding, with counsel" — text or email confirmation counts as intent and shows good faith.
3. **Celebrate the wedding** knowing a postnup is a legitimate, enforceable instrument in most states — and under zero deadline pressure, it's often negotiated *more* honestly than a prenup ever would.

The goal was never the word "prenup" on a specific date. The goal is two people with written clarity before real money merges. If that lands 60 days after the aisle instead of 30 days before, you still won.
