# American-Native Writing Standard (FASE 11–16, 24–26, 41–45)

Every draft passes this standard as a separate editing pass (Level 1 — Editorial).

## FASE 11 — Voice
- Confident but not arrogant.
- Helpful but not patronizing.
- Professional but conversational.
- Clear but not simplistic.
- Neutral but human.
Target: original American English — never translated-sounding, never "AI legal content."

## FASE 12 — Define the reader before writing (concrete, always)
Fill one sentence before drafting:
> "A [age]-year-old American [who is doing X] while [situation], [what they already know/assume]."

Examples: "A 34-year-old American engaged couple researching a prenup before talking to an attorney." · "A business owner considering whether a prenup can protect an existing company." · "Someone getting married for a second time, worried about protecting separate assets."
The reader model lives in the brief's TARGET READER field.

## FASE 13 — Open on the real question
Never open with: "In today's world…" · "When it comes to…" · "Marriage is an important…" · "Planning a wedding is exciting…"
Start with the actual question, tension, or answer the reader came for.

## FASE 14 — Answer first, then expand
Whenever possible: **quick answer → explain → qualify → next steps.**
(Front-loaded answers are how Americans actually read help content.)

## FASE 15 — Human edit pass (separate from drafting)
Read the draft specifically hunting:
- repetition · generic phrases · artificial transitions · heading overload ·
  disclaimer overload · repetitive conclusions · keyword stuffing · robotic language
Rewrite what fails. This pass is mandatory, not optional.

## FASE 16 — AI-fingerprint reduction (goal: write better, not fool detectors)
Eliminate mechanical patterns: "Let's dive in." · "Here's the thing." ·
"Whether you're X or Y…" · "It's important to remember…" ·
"So, what does this mean?" · filler summaries before every section.
If a sentence could appear in any article on any site, cut it.

## FASE 24 — Practical elements (only when they genuinely help)
Checklists · questions to ask · comparisons · decision factors · hypothetical examples · next steps.
Never pad with "practical" widgets that don't serve the reader.

## FASE 25 — Depth = completeness, not word count
Enough information to answer the question completely. NO targets of 1,500/2,000/3,000 words. A tight 700-word answer beats a padded 2,000-word one.

## FASE 26 — Choose the right content type (not everything is a blog post)
| Type | Use for |
|---|---|
| GUIDE | complete explanations |
| QUESTION | direct single-question answers |
| COMPARISON | differences between options |
| CHECKLIST | practical preparation |
| STATE GUIDE | state-specific information |
| COST GUIDE | cost + factors |
| TOOL SUPPORT | helping people use a tool |
Declare the type in the brief (CONTENT TYPE field).

## FASE 41/42 — No fake expertise; real authorship
- Never attribute "attorney / lawyer / legal expert / legal reviewer / specialist" to anyone who isn't one. Francisco Gomes Alves = pastor, writes openly, not a licensed attorney (see `src/data/author.ts` disclaimers).
- No invented personas, no "our legal team," no fake review bylines. Keep the existing transparency architecture.

## FASE 43 — Originality
Every article contributes something of ours: organization · explanation · framework · example · tool · source synthesis. "It exists as a keyword" is not a contribution (FASE 10).

## FASE 44 — Competitor research: learn, don't copy
May scan competitors for: topics · questions · gaps · structure ideas · terminology.
Never copy: text · distinctive phrasing · proprietary graphics · servile imitation of unique structures.

## FASE 45 — Differentiation ("What can PrenupAnswers explain better?")
Our edges: plain-English explanations · practical decision guides · state context · cost transparency · situation-based navigation · built-in tools. Every new piece should use at least one.
